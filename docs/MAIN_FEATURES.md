# TALMA Practice Pal — What Students Experience

This guide focuses on **what learners see and do**. Operational or technical documentation lives elsewhere.

---

## Getting started as a learner

When you open Practice Pal, you land on a **course picker**. Courses are grouped by **organization**—for example, shared community materials appear under **TALMA Community Resources**, while your school or program might appear under its own name with courses tailored to that group.

You pick a course, then browse **lessons** inside it. Each lesson brings together **words to learn**, **speaking activities**, and **games**—depending on how that lesson was built.

Some collections are open to anyone with the link. Others belong to a school or program that asks you to **sign in** first.

**Privacy note:** Optional instant speech checks use your browser’s built-in speech recognition, which may be processed by your browser vendor. Optional voice **recordings** are a separate feature and only saved when your program has enabled that and you have consented.

---

## Multimodal learning (seeing, hearing, speaking)

Practice Pal is built around learning English through **more than reading alone**:

- **Words on screen** — titles, choices, vocabulary, instructions.
- **Pictures** — many vocabulary words and answer choices include images so meaning sticks visually.
- **Audio you listen to** — words and sentences can be read aloud so you hear pronunciation and rhythm.
- **Speaking practice** — in sentence activities you may **repeat or answer aloud**, sometimes with optional recording when your teacher or school has enabled it.
- **Instant speech feedback** — in supported browsers (Chrome/Edge), you can tap **Check my pronunciation** after listening. The browser listens briefly and shows what it heard, with a gentle pass or try-again message. This stays on your device and is separate from optional recording uploads. When your program has voice collection enabled, the last pronunciation-check attempt may also be saved when you tap **Next word**, and progress reflects whether that last check passed.

Together, this supports learners who prefer visuals, listening, or saying things out loud—not only silent clicking.

## Types of activities inside a lesson

Lessons can mix several kinds of practice. Not every lesson includes every type; teachers assemble what fits each unit.

### Vocabulary

Words appear with English text and often **translations** (for example Hebrew or Arabic labels where teachers added them). You typically see **an image per word** and can **hear the word spoken**. Teachers often build vocabulary lists by **importing a spreadsheet**, then the platform helps attach pictures and sounds—so what you see and hear is richer without slower manual setup.

### Sentence prompts (“Practice Pal” style speaking)

You get a question or sentence pattern with **picture choices**. You tap answers and hear prompts read aloud. The activity encourages forming simple sentences that sound natural—similar to guided oral practice in class.

### Matching games

Match pairs—often **words with pictures**, words with meanings, or parallel phrases—in a grid-style memory-and-drag activity.

### Flashcards

Flip-through cards mixing **images**, **English**, and **audio**. Useful for quick review before harder tasks.

### Spelling games

Practice spelling words you’ve studied—often tied directly to that lesson’s vocabulary.

### Sentence builder

Pieces of a sentence are rearranged until you build something grammatical and meaningful—listening and reading work together.

### True/false

Short statements about the vocabulary or topic—you judge whether each one is correct.

### Clause exercises

Fill-in-the-blank style work focused on **grammar chunks** (clauses and phrases), tied to patterns teachers choose.

*(Teachers may also link grammar topics behind the scenes; you experience them inside lesson flows.)*

## Games vs “lesson hub”

The **lesson page** is the home base: vocabulary summaries, links into games, and speaking prompts. **Games** usually open as focused full-screen-style practice so you can concentrate on one skill at a time.

---

## What teachers import—and why it matters for you

Teachers can bring in vocabulary using **structured imports** (such as spreadsheet uploads). From there they can add **illustrations** and **spoken audio** for words. That pipeline means students regularly get:

- Consistent word lists across a unit  
- **Pictures** that reinforce meaning  
- **Hearing every word** without the teacher recording hundreds of clips by hand  

You experience the result as a polished lesson—not as “empty boxes waiting for content.”

---

## Progress and participation

The app can remember **how you interact** with lessons and activities—so teachers and programs can see engagement over time. From your side, it feels like normal browsing and playing; no special steps are required unless your teacher asks you to sign in.

---

## Very short note on access

Some courses are visible to **everyone** in an organization’s catalog. Others are limited to **students in a specific class**. If you don’t see a course your classmates have, your teacher may need to add you to the right group—or you may need to sign in with the account your school gave you.
